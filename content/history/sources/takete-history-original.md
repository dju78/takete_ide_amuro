# Chapter 1 / corrected

To a first-time cosmopolitan visitor, Takete-Ide may retain the trappings of nature. Its largely rustic iron roofs, standing atop mostly unpainted walls interspersed with pockets of modern houses, can ignite inspiration in a curious artist. A single major road from Olorunsogo to Ikoja-Omi, its main artery being at 'Ponna Aiyedayo that runs through it has never been corrupted by tar or concrete since the beginning of time. Several smooth foot paths through which people meander to their respective destinations adorn the terrain.
What can not be taken away is the serenity and scenery of this ancient habitation. A very flat land surrounded by a complex topography of uplands and inhabited by a hospitable, resourceful, and education-loving population.  

Takete-Idera: A People Standing in Comfort

Takete-Idera literally means ‘separated to a place of comfort/rest’. The short form of the suffix- Ide, is now more frequently applied in everyday conversation. Both words are used interchangeably. Irrespective of the form of its appendage, Takete is the name of a peace-loving community in Amuro District of the present MopAmuro Local Government Area in Yagba Federal Constituency of Kogi State. It is located on Latitude 8.16667 and Longitude 6.03333. This location is a distance of 18 kilometres from Mopa, headquarters of the Local Government and 16 kilometres off Kabba-Ilorin road at Effo-Amuro point. 

The village is one of the seven Towns/Villages that make up Amuro District. The others are Orokere, Aiyede, Okagi, Aiyedayo/Iloke, Otafun and Aiyeteju Agbajogun. It is the second largest of the seven villages. In its present site, Takete Ide shares boundary with three out of the remaining six Villages. It is bounded in the east by Otafun Amuro, in the south by Ayedayo Amuro and in the west by Ayede Amuro. This centrality, helped by other innate considerations may have influenced government’s decision to locate an Area Office of the Local Government in this ancient settlement, since 1991 when the council was created by the Babangida, military government (1985-1993)

Amuro is a linguistic contraction of Amu oro, meaning a pot filled with poisons. The people are bound together by common history of warfare, culture and religion. The ancient symbol of the community – a big pot filled with seven poisonous arrows, is believed to be the death-knell of any of the multifarious attackers that marauded the countryside in search of loots, slaves and sometimes simply for pure territorial expansions during the 18th and 19th centuries. Amuro people, like other ethnic stocks in their neighbourhood, are therefore no strangers to wars. 

Each town or village in Amuro has an administration that runs independent of the others, except in areas of defined common interests. Each village has a traditional council headed by the Oba. The Alamuro of Amuro (a title that was created only in 1934) is, however, the head of the Amuro Traditional Council and remains the paramount ruler. He is a graded chief, and by tradition, the Alamuro can not wear a beaded crown unless he is sanctioned by the Ooni of Ife. The number of beaded crowns in Yoruba land is already noted. 

The stool of the Alamuro is rotated among the seven villages. It has no fixed tenure. The holder remains in line. Once an Alamuro dies, the office moves to the next village in line. The seat of the Alamuro is presently in Aiyedayo/Iloke.   

Amuro people are of the Iyagba linguistic/ ethnic stock of northern Yoruba of Kogi west Senatorial Zone. The zone is made up of three federal constituencies, the remaining two being Kabba/ Bunu/ Ijumu and Lokoja/ Kotonkarffe, respectively. Over time, the 'I' in Iyagba has given way and it is now more commonly spelt as Yagba. Both Iyagba land and Kabba/ Bunu/ Ijumu with the Oworo people of Lokoja/ Kotonkarffe are collectively called Okun people, a Yoruba sub-group. Before the state was created on August 27th 1991, Okun people were formerly part of Kabba Province and the old Kwara State, in that order. In fact, Kabba town served as administrative headquarter of that Northern Nigeria Province for a brief period.   

The word okun simply means strength, vitality or power. It is also an expression of goodwill. The people are so named because of their common use of the four letter word 'okun' for salutation. It must be acknowledged however, that Ekitis and Igbominas (in Ekiti and Kwara States respectively) also use this form of greeting. According to Wikipedia, the Okun identity was first suggested by Eva Kraft-Askari during a field expedition in 1965. Okun land has different sub-groups namely Owe, Iyagba, Bunu or Abunu, Ijumu, Kiri, Gbede and Oworo. It is instructive that the various sub-groups share striking historical, cultural and linguistic affinities. Every Okun person almost effortlessly understands the language of another.   Okun people trace their roots to Yoruba land. Okun People and Okunland are historically part of Yotubaland of South West Nigeria.

Origin of Iyagba people 

Yagba Federal Constituency is made up of three Local Government Areas, with a total population of 331,329 (Official Gazette, 2009). They are Yagba-West, Yagba-East and MopAmuro. There are pockets of Yagba speaking people in Ekiti and Kwara States. Yagbaland covers an approximate land area of 3,454 square kilometres and shares boundaries with the Nupe in the North, Ekiti in the South and the Ijumu and Bunu in the South and East respectively. Little or nothing is known about the aboriginal inhabitants of Yagbaland. Even then it is safe to say that tradition is fairly settled that Yagba people migrated from a distant region to settle in their present location. 

The origin narratives on Yabga people were orally conveyed and handed down from one generation unto another. There are various accounts of the migration stories. Two are however more popular and acceptable among scholars than the others. One popular oral tradition account says that the patriarch of the Yagba was a prince from Ile-Ife. Legend holds the ancient town not only as the acceptable dispersal point of all Yoruba but also as the cradle of humanity. 

The Prince whose mission was mainly territorial expansion eventually arrived and settled in Yagba land after many conquests. Everyone dispersed from the Ile-Ife palace was required to bring prompt updates on their mission. But our subject was reported to have returned late to the palace in Ile-Ife to render account of his expedition. 

When he eventually did, he explained how he conquered and lost part of a large territory because he was over-powered by other migrants. Of course, he was blamed for the loss, and he responded impromptu, Iya agba lo jemi (meaning he lost part of the territory because there was no elderly person to counsel him). His listeners laughed, sympathised, and nicknamed him Iya agba. It was from that response that the Iyagba derived their name. The migrant returned to his new territory with a new name that has endured the ages to date. He received and sent messages as Yagba. People of Yagba-east and Mopa-Muro are more at home with the Ile-Ife ancestry.

In the second tradition, Iyagba assumes a new morphology and different semantics. Here, the word is a shrinkage of Iya-agba, meaning an old woman. This account states that the Yagba people are descendants of an old and famous princess from old-Oyo. In her hey days, she was said to have led a group of immigrants in search of economic wellbeing and peace to settle in the new country. Oyo-Ile was said to have been rattled by an external aggressor forcing the Princess to seek relocation to what became ilu Iya agba – town of the old woman, Iyekolo (2000)  quoted by Olayinka Aliu (2013) says the ancestors of Egbe, Odo-Ere, Eri hold firmly to the Oyo-Ile account. 

It has been accepted (Obayemi 1969) that the descents of Iya Agba from Ile Ife occupy Ijumu, Owe, Yagba East and MopAmuro areas, while the one from Oyo Ile occupy Egbe, Ere and Eri group areas (Yagba West).

On the surface, these accounts may appear contradictory and therefore unreliable. But a deeper reflection will immediately reveal striking meeting points. They both confirm that the Yagbas are migrants in their present location. Both traditions also emphasize their Yorubaness. In one narration they came from Ile-Ife and in the other they were from Oyo-Ile. Whichever way, the people are of a strong Yoruba lineage. 

Yagba people are hard working and highly independent minded. In spite of the cultural and historical affinity of the people, they did not have a single all powerful central political authority. Until the period of colonialism, the people were far from being organised into recognisable monarchies. Power resided more with the families and clans. Yagba people are traditionally and predominantly farmers, hunters and warriors.

# Chapter 2

Takete-Ide: Story of a people’s search for peace           

Like the Biblical Jerusalem Takete-Ide today is located on a plain fertile land that is surrounded by a cascade of hilly ground. Oke Elegan to the south stands out in its size and wonderment among them. But smaller hills like Obasoro and Oroke Agodi to the west are high enough to task an ill equipped wayfarer. Of course, the people readily roam the field for game and are at home in this hilly terrain. 

It is a pleasant irony of sorts that the village is also well endowed with one other natural provision- water. Aside few boreholes sunk through government interventions and scanty privileged individuals, Takete-Ide has notable water bodies. Visitors from Ayede (west) axis must first cross the Owowo River to access it. Those from Otafun/Aghara (east) axis must bargain with Eba River. As for those approaching from Ayedayo, (south) their contention will be with Oga. Pockets of streams and springs also dot the landscape. They include Ibedo, Igan, Eba Omosofe, Gboruku, Pandara, and Omi Loke. Apart from serving domestic purposes, the water bodies were rich avenues for the youth to engage in swimming and fishing. The choice of this strikingly hilly mountainous range blended by its aquatic splendour as settlement by her founding fathers could not have been made in a hunch. Let us quickly check out the details.    

Takete-Ide was originally settled in a hilly and rocky terrain and was known and addressed as Ilu –Oke (Village on the hill), south–east of the present location. The community dates back three centuries. There is remarkable evidence of iron-works and brass casting lending credence to its ancient age. 

The people of Takete-Ide started out from a place called Amuro-Odo, meaning settlement of Amuro people. As the name confirms, it was a settlement of Amuro kiths and kin, including the present Itedo Amuro in Isanlu, headquarters of Yagba-East Local Government. In fact, if not for their location, Amuro should have been made up of 8 Towns or Villages. But geographical accidents has dislocated or threatened that ancestral bondage. Nevertheless,Amuro people remain one in brotherhood and spirit. Legend confirms that Amuro people lived together peacefully for ages in Amuro-Odo until the advent of Nupe wars in the 19th century, CE.     

Wars, toils and blood

Okun people occupy the Niger-Benue confluence area along with their neighbours, namely southern Nupe, to the north and to the east, Ebira and Igala. To the west of Okunland are other Yoruba sub-groups Ekiti and Igbominas. Nupe people inhabit the low basin formed by the valleys of the Niger and Kaduna rivers. They were an aggressive block of warriors and slave traders. For most of the later part of the eighteenth century and the early nineteenth century, the Nupe waged an amorphous but relentless war against their neighnours. Not long after the aggression took shape at the turn of the nineteenth century, the well-known Fulani Jihadist, Uthman Dan Fodio, came with his Islamisation message. The Nupes were subsequently incorporated into the Sokoto caliphate, and their war subsumed into the Jihad. 

Nupeland was divided among five emirates, namely Ramah/Bida, Lapai, Agaie,Shonga, and Lafiagi. Femi Kolapo, a researcher on the Nupe wars asserts that the non-Nupe neighbours  mainly the Okun all became entangled in the war and ended up in different levels of political dependency on the attackers, Each of the different groups regularly usurped any of the Okun family. In spite of the courageous defence by the Okun, the devastation was massive. The unfortunate effects of the wars - loss of freedoms, properties, captivity, enslavement, and forced deportation among others did not take time to surface. In any case, for most part of the Nineteenth Century, the entire area that became Nigeria was convulsed in similar wars and turbulence. 

That is the background of what eventually thwarted the long cherished peace of Amuro people and dislocated the community in no small measure. They came on horses with powerful bows and arrows to meet people that least expected war, sometimes in the dead of night, and practically subdued them. Although the aggressors were partly working for the spread of Islam on one hand and raiding for slaves on the other, their method could be likened to the resurfaced modern day banditry ravaging some parts of the country today. They operated as terrorists

Originally, Amuro Odo was located in a fertile plain field, right of the Niger River. This alluring geography made it easy for the aggressors to access and carry out their accursed missions in the area. In short the topography made it smooth for the Nupes to carry out successful raids. As the tales of woes, tears and blood continued, the elders took the inevitable decision to relocate from the beautiful scenery to an area that could offer more protection from the elements. Thus when the area became uncomfortable and absolutely unsafe, the people elected to move from Amuro Odo. This forceful migration saw the people moving in different directions sometimes in groups of two (closest kinsman) or alone. 

Where did the people of Takete go?

As indicated above, the people upon dispersal from Amuro Odo no longer settled in a single location. Instead, they were scattered in different but nonetheless contiguous areas. People that make up the present Takete and kinsmen in present day Otafun, first moved to the east of Amuro Odo to a place called Igbo Ide, which literally means grove or forest of comfort. The place was indeed a very comfortable fertile thick forest in which the people found solace. It was thick and a comfortable vegetation that the people thought they had put the Nupes and their deadly incursions behind them. So peaceful was this new settlement that they simply named it Igbo Idera. It must be emphasised that this first name was used by the two brothers, Takete and Otafun/ Igbo Ide today is a desolate farmland about a few kilometres north of Takete-Ide – Aghara road. Going from Takete-Ide to Otafun, Igbo Ide is on the left, while it is on the right when coming from Otafun to Takete-Ide. 

The new arrivals must have overrated the anticipated succour from their fresh abode. After an undetermined period of peace and economic recovery, the age-long horse riding enemies from Nupe land came calling again. They penetrated the grove and raided it with the ferociousness of an occupation force. They looted the place blind. Once beaten, twice shy. When the enemies took their leave and resumed a process of regular visits for harassments, the people again consulted their legs. In the current flight, however, the people set a new agenda. 

They embarked on comprehensive surveillance and resolved to look for a more fortified landscape. The splendour of the dream location was secondary. What was paramount was security and the need to ward off the Nupe brutality. The people now moved down southward again in search of a rough and upland terrain. They traversed the countryside in search of an ideal location. When they got to the Ibo area, their brothers, Otafun, decided to disengage from the labour and discontinued their journey with the Idera wayfarers. 

History has it that the then leader of the disengaging group was a textile expert. Legend described the man as a specialist in weaving white clothes. He was said to have persuaded the other Idera group to let everyone stay in the hilly area of Otafun. The Takete group remained adamant and said they would separate (Takete) and still look for a more hilly landscape. Subsequently, the people tagged the splinter group Otafunfun or Otafun, meaning weaver or seller of white...There is however another account. In the second narrative, Otafun is a short form of Okuta funfun, which refers to a stone, white stone. Tradition says there was a white stone that attracted the group, and they resolved “let’s go to the white stone to stay”. 

Anyway, the Takete group would not be discouraged from their set task. They continued to advance and over time arrived at a place they named Ilu-Oke, meaning town on the hill, where they preferred to sojourn. In their new settlement, the Nupe raids were no longer as regular and successful as it used to be. The people were also now better prepared against the intruders. Apart from their now more agreeable terrain, which made access to the enemies difficult, especially for horses, the people evolved more potent security measures through purely traditional wisdom. 

After a long spell of relative calmness and wellbeing, their peace was, however, again breached by their former enemies and a new entrant into the battle, Yoruba warriors from Ibadan. Again, the people put up stiff resistance and for a good period, remained impenetrable to the aggressors. However, as their woes had now increased by another leg in the foray, it was only a matter of time for this fortification to crumble. 

With the new threat to peace, they started considering moving further into the mountains. But they were bifurcated almost down the centre this time. Those who advocated fresh exodus eventually carried the day and proceeded to Okeata, a new location around Odede area. A small group, chiefly the Eseyin Meleri stayed back and took over the land. From then on, they controlled the entire area from Okuta gboro to Oke Eba through Agbara gidan down to Ibo and gheain the Otafun boarder. It was most probably around this time that the people invented and found solace in Agbagba Ide- a protective custody of no mean magic. 

The Agbagba Ide was a cave that offered the people unfailing protection from their enemies. According to tradition, once an endangered soul can approach its vicinity, the danger must pass him by. The people concluded in their native intelligence that there was something mysterious and mystical about the Agbagba. They were convinced it must have been sent from Orun, heaven, the abode of Olorun or Olodumare, God to give them complete deliverance from their marauders. The people had an esoteric means of appeasing the Agbagba Ide. The Nupes and the Ibadans permanently lost the type of fatal threats they used to pose. Their brutality no longer counted. As they aggressed, they suffered bloodied noses and serial defeats from a people they once humiliated. 

Realising the great potential of this mystery and in recognition of its enormous powers to deliver from the enemy, they devised its movable representation or prototype. It was not unlikely that the people sometimes took the prototype to battle to scare away enemies, something similar to the way Jews of the Old Testament used the Ark of the Covenant as recorded in the scriptures. It is, therefore, little wonder that the Agbagba Ide is the symbol of Takete Ide. The Agbagba Ide today is still believed to be a strong source of protection by Olorun for Takete-Ide people. As the wars were winding to a close, many of the groups that went to Okeata returned to Iluoke. But the Eseyin telu group opted not to go back. Impressed by the fertility of their new arable terrain, they took over the land farming all along the present Takete Ide. Their farming activities extended all the way from Odede, through Igbola to a small stream after an Oparun vegetation along Iluke road.

The new site: Home at last             
That was how the wanderings went in the migration narratives of our forefathers. A man named Thomas Ode is credited to be the first progenitor to settle in the present location. As the name suggests, Pa. Thomas was a hunter. In those days when wild animals and elemental forces ruled the jungle, and even up to date, hunters were not ordinary beings. They were partly professional seers, sorcerers, and herbalists. They roamed the jungles adequately fortified with one tool of divination or the other. Pa. Thomas’ fame in clairvoyance was well known. For instance, he was said to have possessed the mystical power to turn an anthill into an antelope or any such animal whenever he suffered a lulll in his hunting engagements in the jungle.

While on a hunting expedition through the forest, Pa Thomas was said to have consulted the oracle as a routine. As he poured the Obi Ifa, a prophetic instrument made of cola nuts, it was immediately revealed to him the formidable nature of the land on which he trod. He repeated the inquest several times over, receiving the same verdict. Each time, he got the same message that the place was not only good for habitation but would never fall under enemy attack.

Elated and promptly, Pa Thomas took the good news back to Iluoke and principally to his co-hunters. His colleagues were joyous about the revelation. And even though they did not doubt its veracity, they decided to seek further divine opinion as confirmation. Again, this select and respected band put the necessary things in motion to access the mind of Divinity on the matter. Without delay, it was revealed to each and every one of them the same message that had been given to their forerunner. 

Once verification was obtained and circulated in the countryside, the people began their downward journey from the hill to the plain that flowed with milk and honey. Odo Takete promptly descended to its present location. Oke Adan also did the same. Oke Ako had a stop-over at Aiyetoro, near Agbaraunkule, before eventually joining their kiths and kin in their present location. The migrations from the uplands to the low plains took place in 1926 or thereabouts, according to records. The first child to be born in the new location was Pa. George Fiki. It is somehow fortuitous that his younger brother, Joshua was to emerge  several decades later first as the Olude, and later as the Alamuro. Oba Joshua fiki reigned from 198× - 200×

The people have since their relocation been living together in unbroken love and common brotherhood. It is now nearly a century, and no records of communal suspicion, let alone war have been heard among them.

The name initially given to the new location was Ileteju or flat land. The name carried within it an acknowledgement of the people’s recent hectic experience that turned them to mountain dwellers. Ileteju was fertile and beautiful. The environment had also thus far been very peaceful. Gone were the devastating horses and their hostile riders. Farms yielded plentiful as they no longer witnessed the very rampant and destructive trespass of the horses. In perfect fulfilment of the prophecies, a moment of prosperity, calmness and peace was palpable in the air. 

The people had complete vacation from their woes. In fact, they now dwelt once again in absolute if not heavenly comfort. Ileteju no longer sufficiently reflected their new and pleasant station in life. They needed a fresh identity. After subjecting the matter to some form of public discussion, they reached for their bag of names and carefully coined a new appellation, Takete-Idera, which reflected everything sensational, surreal, energetic, independent and wholesome. It is a cognomen that has stayed alive to its meaning over the ages.
